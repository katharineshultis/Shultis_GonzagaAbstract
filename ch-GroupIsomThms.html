    Chapter 16 Group Isomorphism Theorems

    Objectives: The learning outcomes in this chapter are to:

    • ...be able to state all three isomorphism theorems for groups.
      🔗
    • ...be able to use the group isomorphism theorems in examples.
      🔗
    • ...understand the need in many proofs for showing that the maps are well-defined.
      🔗
    🔗
    There are three isomorphism theorems (telling us that creating certain algebraic structures always results in isomorphic structures) and they hold for many different kinds of algebraic structures. In this chapter, we’ll focus on the group versions of the theorems. First, we’ll need a definition:
    🔗

    Definition 16.0.1.

    Let \(G\) and \(H\) be groups and \(\varphi:G\to H\) be a group homomorphism. The kernel of \(\varphi\) is the subset of \(G\) defined as the set of stuff that maps to the appropriate identity, i.e.
    \begin{gather*} \ker(\varphi)=\{g\in G\mid \varphi(g)=e_H\} \end{gather*}
    where \(e_H\) denotes the identity element in \(H\text{.}\) Also, recall that the image of \(\varphi\) is the subset of \(H\) defined as
    \begin{gather*} \operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\varphi(G)=\{\varphi(g)\mid g\in G\}=\{h\in H\mid h=\varphi(g)\text{ for some }g\in G\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We’ve done less with groups than we have with rings so far, and the following facts could be useful in your proofs. The proofs of these facts are similar to the proofs of the similar facts for rings; however, I neither want to include them in lecture, nor ask you to put them in your portfolio. Maybe make Dr. Shultis prove them in class some time soon!?
    🔗

    Proposition 16.0.2.

    Let \(G\) and \(H\) be groups written multiplicatively
     1 
    for notational convenience
    and \(\varphi:G\to H\) a group homomorphism.
    1. If \(e_G\) and \(e_H\) denote the identity elements of \(G\) and \(H\) respectively, then \(\varphi(e_G)=e_H\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. For any \(g\in G\text{,}\) we have \(\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(g)^{-1}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 16.0.3. First Isomorphism Theorem for Groups.

    Let \(G\) and \(H\) be groups and \(\varphi:G\to H\) a group homomorphism. Then \(\ker(\varphi)\) is a normal subgroup of \(G\text{,}\) \(\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\) is a subgroup of \(H\) and
    \begin{gather*} G/\ker(\varphi)\cong\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We have already abused multiplication notation by using it between two things that aren’t actually group elements. We did this when we wrote \([a]\) as \(aH\text{.}\) This is also similar to the abuse in notation from Quotient Rings. Taken literally, this is nonsense if \(a\) is a group element and \(H\) is a subgroup (i.e. a set of elements). But we just meant
    \begin{gather*} aH=\{ah:h\in H\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    Similarly, we will further abuse notation to "multiply" two sets of group elements \(A\subseteq G\) and \(B\subseteq G\) by multiplying every combination of their elements:
    \begin{gather*} AB=\{ab:a\in A,b\in B\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗

    Theorem 16.0.4. Second Isomorphism Theorem for Groups.

    Let \(G\) be a group written multiplicatively
     2 
    for notational convenience
    and \(A\text{,}\) \(B\) subgroups of \(G\) with \(B\lhd G\text{.}\) Also, let \(AB\) denote the subset \(\{ab\mid a\in A, b\in B\}\) of \(G\text{.}\) Then, \(AB\) is a subgroup of \(G\text{,}\) \(B\) is a normal subgroup of \(AB\text{,}\) \(A\cap B\) is a normal subgroup of \(A\text{,}\) and
    \begin{gather*} A/(A\cap B)\cong (AB)/B\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 16.0.5. Third Isomorphism Theorem for Groups.

    Let \(G\) be a group, \(H\subseteq K\subseteq G\) with \(H,K\) normal subgroups of \(G\text{.}\) Then, \(K/H\) is a normal subgroup of \(G/H\) and
    \begin{gather*} (G/H)/(K/H)\cong G/K\text{.} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 16.0.6.

    Let \(X\) be a (probably finite for now) set. Let \(F(X)\) denote the set of all "words" in the elements of \(X\) and their "inverses". So some examples of elements of \(F(\{a,b,c,d,e\})\) include \(abbca^{-1}d^{-1}\) and \(ada^{-1}d^{-1}db\text{.}\) Let \(\star\) be an operation on \(F(X)\) defined by concatenation so that for our example, \(abbca^{-1}d^{-1}\star ada^{-1}d^{-1}db=abbca^{-1}d^{-1}ada^{-1}d^{-1}db\text{.}\) Note also that we typically don’t write \(x\) and \(x^{-1}\) immediately next to one another since they’re inverse of each other and so cancel out. Also, when we have a string of several \(x\)’s in a row, we typically use exponential notation, so \(aaa=a^3\) and \(b^{-1}b^{-1}=b^{-2}\text{,}\) and our product above should be simplified to \(ab^2ca^{-1}d^{-1}ada^{-1}b\text{.}\) Then, \(F(X)\) is a group under this operation and is called the free group on \(X\).
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 16.0.7.

    You have already seen the free group in the exercises. Indeed, Dr. Hogle added Exercise 11B.iii to the notes, and introduced us there to the free group on the set \(X=\{x,y\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 16.0.8. A Quotient of a Free Group - Introducing Generators and Relations.

    Let \(G=\{r,f\}\) and let \(F\) be the free group on \(G\text{.}\) Then, let \(N\) be the normal subgroup of \(F\) generated by \(R=\{f^2,r^3,fr(r^2f)^{-1}\}\text{.}\) Then \(F/N\cong D_3\text{.}\) Indeed, define a homomorphism \(\varphi: F\to D_3\) via \(r\mapsto r\) and \(f\mapsto f\text{.}\) Then, \(r^3,f^2,fr(r^2f)^{-1}\in \ker(\varphi)\) which means that \(N\subseteq\ker(\varphi)\) as \(N\) is the normal subgroup of \(F\) generated by \(r^3\text{,}\) \(f^2\text{,}\) and \(fr(r^2f)^{-1}\text{.}\) It can also be shown that \(\ker(\varphi)\subseteq N\text{.}\) Moreover, as \(\operatorname{im}\varphi=D_3\text{,}\) then \(F/N\cong D_3\text{.}\) We call \(G\) the set of generators of \(D_3\) and \(R\) the set of relations of \(D_3\)
     3 
    Hence, the notational choices!
    . In fact, every group can be constructed in this way with a set of generators and relations! However, we’ll leave that as an unproven fact for now.
    🔗

    Aside: More on Generators and Relations.

    The \(G\) is for "generators" and the \(R\) is for "relations." Moreover, when we say \(N\) is the normal subgroup generated by \(R\text{,}\) we mean that \(N\) is the smallest set in \(F\) which contains \(R\) and is a normal subgroup of \(F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises

    16.A.

    Prove the First Isomorphism Theorem for Groups. The steps below should guide your proof.
    1. Show that \(\ker(\varphi)\) is a normal subgroup of \(G\text{.}\) Note that this requires proving that \(\ker(\varphi)\) is a subgroup and that it’s normal. We, unfortunately, don’t have a "normal subgroup test."
      🔗
    2. Show that \(\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\) is a subgroup of \(H\) (presumably using the subgroup test).
      🔗
    3. Define the "obvious" function to use here \(f:G/\ker(\varphi)\to\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\text{.}\) As you may not yet have enough experience to have an instinct as to what the "obvious" function is, I’d be happy to have a whole class discussion about ideas/suggestions that you do have.
      🔗
    4. Since elements of \(G/\ker(\varphi)\) are cosets (and hence typically have multiple coset representatives), we’ll need to show the function we wrote down is "well-defined." That is, using \(N\) to denote \(\ker(\varphi)\text{,}\) we need that for any \(gN=g'N\text{,}\) we have \(f(gN) = f(g'N)\text{.}\) If this step fails, that’s a good hint that our function in the previous step is wrong.
      🔗
    5. Now that we know \(f\) is a well-defined function between the two groups we want to show are isomorphic, we need to show that \(f\) is a group homomorphism. This step should be straight-forward. Use the definition of a homomorphism and the way that \(f\) is defined.
      🔗
    6. We also need to show that \(f\) is a bijection.
      🔗

    16.B.

    These are some basic exercises on using the isomorphism theorems.
    1. Prove that \(Q_8/Z\cong \Z/2\Z\times\Z/2\Z\) using the First Isomorphism Theorem.
      🔗
    2. Let \(F\) be a field and let \(1\) denote the multiplicative identity in \(F\text{.}\) The notation \(GL_2(F)\) denotes the group of \(2\times 2\) matrices with nonzero determinant and entries in \(F\text{.}\) The notation \(SL_2(F)\) denotes the subgroup of \(GL_2(F)\) where the matrices in \(GL_2(F)\) have determinant \(1\text{.}\) The notation \(F^\ast\) denotes the nonzero elements of \(F\text{,}\) i.e. the elements of \(F\) which have multiplicative inverses. Lastly, the notation \(F^\ast\cdot I_2\) denotes the \(2\times 2\) matrices of the form
      \begin{align*} \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix} \end{align*}
      with \(a\in F^\ast\text{.}\) Use the Second Isomorphism Theorem for Groups to show that \(GL_2({\mathbb C})/({\mathbb C}^\ast\cdot I)\cong SL_2({\mathbb C})/\{\pm I_2\}\) where \(\{\pm I_2\}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\right\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Note that \(H=\{(1),(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}\) is a normal subgroup of \(S_4\) which is contained in
      \begin{gather*} A_4=\{(1),(123),(132),(124),(142),(134),(143),(234),(243),(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}\text{.} \end{gather*}
      We will spend some time in the next chapter learning more about the notation for the symmetric groups, \(S_n\text{.}\) In this exercise, use the Third Isomorphism Theorem for Groups with these groups to prove a new isomorphism. This should be doable even without the knowledge you will learn in the next chapter.

    16.C.

    Prove the Second Isomorphism Theorem for Groups. To prove it, you’ll need to show that \(AB\) is a subgroup of \(G\text{,}\) \(A\) is a normal subgroup of \(AB\text{,}\) and \(A\cap B\) is a normal subgroup of \(A\text{.}\) You’ll then need to go about showing the claimed isomorphism holds. To show that isomorphism, you can either appeal to Theorem 16.0.3, or write down a function and show it is a (well-defined) bijective homomorphism.
    🔗
    🔗

    16.D.

    Prove the Third Isomorphism Theorem for Groups. To prove it, you’ll need to show that \(H/K\) is a normal subgroup of \(G/K\text{.}\) You’ll then need to go about showing the claimed isomorphism holds. To show that isomorphism, you can either appeal to Theorem 16.0.3, or write down a function and show it is a (well-defined) bijective homomorphism.
