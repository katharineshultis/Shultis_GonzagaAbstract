    Chapter 18 What is a Monoid?

    Objectives: The learning outcomes for this chapter are to...

    • ...understand the definition of a monoid.
      🔗
      🔗
    • ...be able to give several examples of monoids.
      🔗
      🔗
    • ...understand some basic properties of monoids.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    As promised, I’m going to continue taking properties away from you. While it’s certainly possible to take away associativity, I’m less well versed in such algebraic structures. Instead, the next property I’m taking away from you is the existence of inverses. Here’s our definition:
    🔗

    Definition 18.0.1.

    A monoid \((M,\star)\) is a set \(M\) together with an operation \(\star\) such that:
    (A)
    🔗
    The operation is associative.
    🔗
    (Id)
    🔗
    The operation has an identity element, denoted \(e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 18.0.2.

    As is probably unsurprising, but possibly worth stating anyways, an element \(m\) in a monoid \((M,\star)\) is said to be invertible with inverse \(m'\) if \(m\star m'=m'\star m=e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 18.0.3.

    Note that every group satisfies the conditions for being a monoid, so every group is a monoid.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 18.0.4.

    Recall that the multiplicative requirements on a ring (ignoring the existence of addition) is simply that multiplication is associative. However, in any ring with identity, the multiplication operation satisfies the conditions of being a monoid, so every ring with identity is a monoid under multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 18.0.5.

    Don’t forget here that in order for a ring to be a group, we insisted that we were looking at the addition operation. Also, we were able to turn the multiplication of any field into a group, but only by first removing the zero element from the set.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 18.0.6.

    Which of our “favorite” sets, \(\N,\Z,\Q,\R,{\mathbb C}\) are monoids under addition? Under multiplication?
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 18.0.7.

    Let \(X\) be any nonempty set and consider the power set \(\mathcal{P}(X)\text{.}\) Note that \(\cup\) and \(\cap\) are both operations on \(\mathcal{P}(X)\) as the intersection and union of any two subsets of \(X\) is again a subset of \(X\text{.}\) Do either of these operations turn \(\mathcal{P}(X)\) into a monoid?
    🔗
    🔗
    Rather than having chapters for associated objects to monoids, we’re going to do all of monoids in one chapter. It should be no surprise that the next definition is one of a submonoid. It should also come as no surprise that a submonoid is a “sub-monoid”, i.e. a subset of a monoid that is also a monoid. Regardless, here’s a more formal definition.
    🔗

    Definition 18.0.8.

    Let \((M,\star)\) be a monoid. A set \(N\) is a submonoid of \(M\) if:
    (S)
    🔗
    The set \(N\) is a (nonempty) subset of \(M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Cl)
    🔗
    If \(a,b\in N\text{,}\) then so is \(a\star b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (M)
    🔗
    The set \(N\) together with the operation \(\star\) is a monoid. That is, it satisfies the axioms of a monoid:
    (A)
    🔗
    The operation is associative.
    🔗
    (Id)
    🔗
    The operation has an identity element, denoted \(e\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 18.0.9.

    Suppose you’re looking at addition on \(\Z\text{.}\) In order for a subset \(A\) to be a monoid, you’ll need \(0\in A\text{.}\) If you also have \(5\in A\text{,}\) then you know that you’ll need \(10=5+5\in A\) and \(15=10+5\in A\text{.}\) It probably doesn’t surprise you that \(\{5n\mid n\in\Z,n\geq 0\}\) is the submonoid generated by 5, i.e. the smallest submonoid of \(\Z\) containing \(\{5\}\text{.}\) What happens if you know that \(3,5\in A\text{?}\) What are all the elements of the submonoid generated by \(\{3,5\}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 18.0.10.

    There’s certainly more that we could put in the chapter about monoids including quotients, homomorphisms, isomorphisms, isomorphism theorems, etc. However, I’m going to put some of that in the optional exercises and skip the rest of it since these are less well studied for monoids than they are for many other algebraic structures.
    🔗
    🔗

    1. Do you typically consider \(0\) to be a natural number? Does your answer to either part Item 18.18.A.i or Item 18.18.A.ii of Exercise Exercise 18.18.A change if you change your mind about whether or not \(0\) is natural?

Consider the set \(\Z\times\Z\) together with an operation \(\$\) defined by \((x_{1},y_{1})\$(x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{1}x_{2}+2y_{1}y_{2},x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1})\) where the addition and multiplication on the right are the typical operations in \(\Z\text{.}\) Show that this is a monoid, making sure to identify the identity element. Also show that we have cancellation of nonzero elements in this monoid, meaning if \((x_{1},y_{1})\neq (0,0)\text{,}\) show that \((x_{1},y_{1})\$(x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{1},y_{1})\$(x_{3},y_{3})\) implies that \((x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{3},y_{3})\text{.}\)

    18.C.

    Certainly we might want to have the notion of a quotient monoid. In order to do this, we need an equivalence relation \(\sim\) on our monoid \(M\) such that if \(a,a',b,b'\in M\) and \(a\sim a'\) and \(b\sim b'\text{,}\) then \(ab\sim a'b'\text{.}\) Show that this turns the set of equivalence classes into a monoid. Then, identify a condition on a submonoid \(N\) so that the equivalence relation is found by taking cosets of \(N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    18.D.

    Let \(M\) and \(N\) be monoids, both written multiplicatively. A function \(\varphi:M\to N\) is a monoid homomorphism if \(\varphi(m_{1}m_{2})=\varphi(m_{1})\varphi(m_{2})\) for any \(m_{1},m_{2}\in M\text{,}\) and \(\varphi(e_{M})=e_{N}\) where \(e_{M}\) and \(e_{N}\) are the identity elements of \(M\) and \(N\text{,}\) respectively. It should come as no surprise that we say two monoids are isomorphic if there is a bijective monoid homomorphism between them. Consider the set \(\Z\) together with the operation \(\heartsuit\) defined by \(a\heartsuit b=a+b-ab\) where the operations on the right are the standard ones in \(\Z\text{.}\) Show that \(\Z\) is a monoid under \(\heartsuit\) with identity element \(0\) and that the function \(\varphi:\Z\to\Z\) given by \(a\mapsto 1-a\) is a monoid isomorphism between the monoid \(\Z\) under multiplication and \(\Z\) under the operation \(\heartsuit\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    18.E.

    Let \(M\) be a monoid written multiplicatively and \(a\in M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Suppose that there are elements \(b,c\in M\) such that \(ab=1\) and \(ca=1\) (i.e. \(b\) is a right inverse of \(a\) and \(c\) is a left inverse of \(a\)). Show that \(b=c\) (and hence \(a\) is invertible with inverse \(a^{-1}=b\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Show that \(a\) is invertible with inverse \(b\) if and only if \(aba=a\) and \(ab^{2}a=1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    18.F.

    Consider the “Chicken nugget problem” which was asked on the NPR program Car Talk:
    🔗
    “A famous fast-food restaurant sells chicken nuggets. They come in three sizes, a box of \(6\text{,}\) a box of \(9\text{,}\) or a box of \(20\text{.}\) You order chicken nuggets by the box. If you’re moderately hungry you might buy a box of \(9\text{,}\) and if there’s more than one of you, you might buy a box of \(20\) and divide them up. Using these order sizes, you can order, for example, \(32\) pieces of chicken if you wanted. You’d order a box of \(20\) and two boxes of \(6\text{.}\) But you can’t purchase exactly \(31\) chicken nuggets. Might there be a larger number for which an exact order is not possible? Here’s our question of the day: What is the largest number of chicken nuggets that you cannot order exactly?”
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s your task as a student in Math 438 working on this problem. First, solve the chicken nugget problem, making sure to explain and fully justify your answer. Then, explain what this has to do with monoids.
